# Feature guide

What built features look like in a bare-ground model, how big they usually are, and the look-alikes to rule out. Read it before looking at tiles. When you mark a find, start its note with the entry it matches, for example "Guide: burial mound." When nothing fits, say "Guide: no entry" and describe the shape.

Sizes are typical, not limits. Sky view shows banks bright and ditches and hollows dark whatever their direction; hillshade shows height but can hide a bank that runs along the light.

## Built features that survive as relief

- **Burial mound.** A round dome, 5 to 40 m across and 0.3 to 5 m high. Often in groups or rows. May have a dark ring (a quarry ditch) around it, and a small dark pit or dent at the top where it was dug into. Mark each mound as its own find.
- **Platform mound.** A flat top with straight, steep sides, square or rectangular, 20 to 100 m or more across, sometimes with a ramp or a lower step.
- **Bank and ditch, enclosure.** A continuous bright line with a dark line beside it, closing a circle, an oval or a square from 30 m to a kilometre across. Gaps are entrances. Mark the work once, not each stretch.
- **Hillfort ramparts.** Several banks and ditches one inside another, following the contour of a hilltop, with entrances that often zigzag through outworks.
- **Terraces.** Flat steps along a slope, parallel to the contours, each a few metres to tens of metres wide.
- **Hollow way.** A sunken track 2 to 10 m wide and 0.5 to 3 m deep, often several side by side, running between places or down a slope.
- **Field system.** Low banks dividing the ground into plots: small rectangles of 30 to 100 m with soft banks (prehistoric fields), or long parallel ridges 5 to 15 m apart in a gentle reversed S (ridge and furrow).
- **Charcoal hearth.** A flat round platform 8 to 15 m across cut into a wooded slope, with a slight bank on the downhill side. Often dozens in one forest.
- **House platform or pit house.** A flat rectangle 5 to 20 m across, or a round or square hollow of the same size, often in a row or a cluster.
- **Kiln, mine or quarry working.** A pit with a heap beside it, or a line of them along a seam.

## Look-alikes to rule out

- **Sinkhole.** A round hollow with steep sides and no bank, often several in limestone country. Not built.
- **Tree throw.** A pit 2 to 5 m across with a low heap on one side, scattered at random through woodland, usually many. Not built.
- **Bomb or shell crater.** A round crater 5 to 15 m across with a raised rim of thrown earth, often in lines or clusters. Modern.
- **Prospect pit or modern quarry.** A pit with fresh, sharp edges and a spoil heap; usually beside a track. Modern unless it is in a line along a seam with soft edges.
- **Drainage ditch.** Straight or gently curving, even width, joined to a stream or another ditch. Modern.
- **Plough lines and field edges.** Straight parallel striping that stops at a field boundary. Modern.
- **Track, trail or logging road.** Long, even width, joined to other tracks, cut into a slope on one side and built out on the other. Usually modern.
- **Spoil heap or dump.** An irregular heap beside a quarry, a building or a road. Modern.
- **Erosion gully and river forms.** Branching channels, river terraces and levees that follow the water. Natural.
- **Glacial and wind forms.** Drumlins, eskers and dunes: smooth, elongated, in parallel sets over large areas. Natural.
- **Modern earthworks.** Building pads, ponds, dams, golf course mounds, silage clamps. Modern. A reconstructed or restored ancient mound is still the ancient feature.
- **Data artefacts.** Straight bands from flight lines, flat facets where the laser did not reach the ground, hard edges at the survey's border. Say so; do not mark them.

## When unsure

Look again in the other view. A real mound is round in both; a tree throw has its heap on one side. A real bank keeps its line across changes in slope; a track joins other tracks. If still unsure, record the tile as possible and say why, rather than marking a find.
